CHAPTER ONE
INTRODUCTION
1.1 Background to the Study
Enhancing the learning environment in Nigerian universities has been discussed for years, and technology remains a compelling solution. As with many Federal Universities in Nigeria, the student population at Federal University Lokoja (FULokoja) is increasing, classrooms are limited, lecturers teach large classes, and students do not always have easy access to learning materials. It is not uncommon to see students crowding around a single printed page to take photos or photocopies, or to miss an announcement because it was sent in a WhatsApp group they were not subscribed to. Learning Management Systems (LMS) are electronic platforms that integrate teaching, learning resources, communication, and assessment in a single location; Waykar and Gadekar (2023) define an LMS as a single source for handling course content, administration, communication, and assessment. This is significant at FULokoja because learners currently rely on printed notes that are shared, photographed, or passed through WhatsApp, email, Xender, Bluetooth, and Airdrop, none of which ensures that all students receive the same material at the same time or in the same format.
In 2025, FULokoja introduced Moodle in the Computer Science Department, training twenty-three lecturers and launching the platform at lms.fulokoja.edu.ng to support open distance and blended learning (Adewumi et al., 2025). The pilot successfully launched the system and captured lecturers' interest, but at the time of the study, many lecturers were still uploading course materials, and the project focused on lecturers' skills rather than the student experience or accessibility. This mirrors the wider Nigerian picture: Mohammed and Opeyemi (2020) found that lecturers at Ahmadu Bello University, Zaria were hindered from using Moodle mainly by a lack of technical support and awareness, while Sulaiman and Abubakar (2024) found a strong correlation between LMS use and effective teaching across five Nigerian universities, alongside barriers such as poor digital literacy, unstable internet, and weak institutional support. Only a few study in Nigeria have measured a direct academic impact, but not opinion, and in both Oguguo et al. (2021) found that the students taught using Moodle at Imo State University performed significantly higher when compared to those taught with another package and Ojo (2024) found that the students taught with Moodle at Ajayi Crowther University performed better than those taught with another package, though the platform was underutilized due to lack of policy to support its regular use and lack of training of the lecturers. Taken together, all these studies converge on the same point: the problem is not just the technology, but whether it is the right technology for the way that a particular institution teaches and learns; whether the technology is used in ongoing lectures; and whether training is provided.
LMS adoption has also risen sharply worldwide; Sanchez et al. (2024), comparing forty-five platforms, found that one major online learning platform grew from twenty-six million users in 2016 to one hundred and eighty-nine million in 2021, while Salah and Thabet (2021) found that open-source systems like Moodle, Chamilo and Open edX now compare favourably with expensive commercial systems, which matters for a public university like FULokoja with limited funds. Learning Management Systems also gained significance during COVID-19 by enabling teaching to continue during lockdown (Benjamen, 2025), a benefit equally relevant to FULokoja students who miss class due to illness, disability, or other personal reasons.
Although this project targets students with disabilities, an LMS can help all students. Elsewhere, LMS platforms support students on industrial attachment, those in online or blended programs, and those who cannot attend all lectures due to work. FULokoja does not run these forms of learning at scale yet, but a system built to support them would leave the University better equipped for its future needs.
As I reflect on accessibility for an LMS, I would say that there are two things that this project revealed and two things that it did not. I did not meet any blind or deaf students in casual conversations with lecturers or students in the Computing, Life Sciences and Education faculties; the only people I identified were a number of students with physical impairments affecting their mobility. But rather than writing off accessibility as a minor concern, I think the very fact that I did not see blind and deaf students on campus itself is an issue that demands attention. Irizarry (2026) demonstrated that adding accessibility functions to an LMS improves the use of digital learning materials within one semester. Thus, even though I did not meet any blind or deaf students during this study, it is reasonable and necessary to design the proposed system with accessibility features so that such students can use it without difficulty if admitted in the future, and so that any who are already here but not visible because of existing barriers are still supported.
According to Muntari and Abdullahi (2025), improving education in Nigeria cannot be achieved in a small way; teaching and learning must be given and managed differently. This mentality guided this project's focus on a system that supports FULokoja's daily learning experience, replacing dependence on WhatsApp groups and handout pictures, supporting learners beyond the standard full-time on-campus student, and building in accessibility from the start so that a student with disabilities can access learning materials without unnecessary barriers.
1.2 Statement of the Problem
At the present time, Federal University Lokoja has piloted a Moodle-based LMS through the Computer Science Department since 2025, and a group of twenty-three lecturers had been trained to use it, but the platform has not yet been made university-wide, and lecturers were still in the process of uploading course materials months after the pilot began (Adewumi et al., 2025). According to Sulaiman and Abubakar (2024), the primary challenges affecting LMS adoption across Nigeria's universities are poor digital literacy, infrastructure issues, and weak institutional support, while Ojo (2024) also observed that the presence of an LMS does not by itself benefit students if lecturers do not take part in its use and the institution has no clear ICT strategy in place. Neither the FULokoja pilot nor these wider Nigerian studies address accessibility. Students still receive materials as printed documents to photograph or photocopy, or through WhatsApp, email, Xender, Bluetooth or Airdrop as per each lecturer's preference, and none of these methods are readable by screen reader software, provide transcripts for videos, or offer an audio-format alternative to written material. From these results and the experience of the pilot, what FULokoja needs is a Moodle-based system built around how students are now getting learning materials, one that is accessible to disabled students without special modification and is easy for lecturers to use on top of what the departmental pilot has already shown is achievable.
1.3 Aim and Objectives of the Study
This project aims to design, extend, and evaluate a Moodle-based Integrated Learning Management System (ILMS) for Federal University Lokoja.
The specific objectives of the study are:
I. To explore the current manner of course delivery in the Federal University, Lokoja and consider related Learning Management Systems, particularly the existing Moodle environment.
II. To create a Moodle-based Integrated Learning Management System for Federal University Lokoja which will have features to enable the management of courses, communication and assessment.
III. To integrate accessibility features that cater for students who are hearing and mobility impaired, while also improving access for all learners.
IV. To test and evaluate the proposed system and make recommendations for its implementation at Federal University Lokoja.
1.4 Significance of the Study
The importance of this project is that it will enable Federal University Lokoja to use a single, unified Moodle-based platform for course management, communication, assessment, and accessibility. In contrast to the current Moodle pilot, which was largely centered on lecturer training and deployment, this study emphasizes student usability and the accessibility issues that the pilot did not address. It also contributes to the growing body of literature on LMS in Nigeria, since previous studies have not paid much attention to accessibility, and the system's features for students with visual, hearing, and mobility impairments are in line with the objectives of the Nigerian National Policy on Education and the Discrimination against Persons with Disabilities (Prohibition) Act, 2018.
The design approach and accessibility features developed in this study can also serve as a guide for other Nigerian universities planning to implement or improve their own Moodle-based learning environments. Lastly, the system is useful to students in a practical way, allowing them to access learning materials no matter where they are or if they have a disability, continue learning without hassle if they miss lectures for any reason, and enrol in future knowing accessibility features are already in place.
1.5 Scope of the Study
The study is centred on the design and implementation of a Moodle-based Integrated Learning Management System for Federal University Lokoja, including screen reader compatibility, alternative text for images, video captioning, high contrast mode, keyboard navigation, and adjustable text size, alongside course management, communication, assignment submission and grading, announcements, and progress tracking. The study also involved a review of the existing Moodle pilot and a discussion with students on the current method of course delivery at FULokoja, and the proposed system was designed and tested within the FULokoja environment. The study does not address full university-wide deployment of the prototype, as this would require institutional-level approval and resources not included in this study.
1.6 Limitations of the Study
Due to time constraints for a final year project, the scale of system development and user testing was limited, and the data collected from students and lecturers comes from informal discussions rather than the opinions of the whole university. Unreliable internet connectivity at FULokoja also impacted development and testing. A further restriction concerns accessibility: no currently enrolled blind or deaf student was identified during the research, and only a few students with mobility challenges were available, so screen reader compatibility and video captioning were tested using standard accessibility guidelines and testing tools rather than direct testing by blind and deaf users. Additional testing with users with different disabilities would be necessary to fully validate the system's accessibility.
1.7 Definition of Terms
I. Learning Management System (LMS): Software that provides, manages, and monitors instruction, communications, assessment, and administration of learning courses and activities in a single environment (Benjamen, 2025).
II. Accessibility: Making digital environments easy to access and use for students with visual, hearing, cognitive or mobility impairments (Irizarry, 2026).
III. WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines): Technical guidelines from the World Wide Web Consortium that outline how digital platforms are built to be accessible, it is considered the international standard for the design of accessible LMS (Irizarry, 2026).
IV. Open Distance Learning (ODL): A mode of academic delivery where students can access academic content and engage with lecturers mostly online, instead of having to attend class physically, as they had in the pilot conducted by FULokoja, Adewumi et al (2025).
V. Moodle: Moodle is an open-source LMS platform that was first launched in 2002 and is considered the most widely adopted free LMS in the world (Salah & Thabet, 2021); it was the platform used in the pilot in FULokoja 2025 by Adewumi et al. (2025).
VI. TPCK (Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge): A combination of technological competence, pedagogical skill, and subject matter expertise that determines the effectiveness of a lecturer's use of digital learning tools (Sulaiman & Abubakar, 2024).

CHAPTER TWO
LITERATURE REVIEW
2.1 Theoretical Background
2.1.1 Introduction to the General Subject Area
A Learning Management System (LMS) is a software platform that integrates course materials, communication, assessment, and academic management to streamline teaching and learning processes and track student progress (Waykar & Gadekar, 2023). Unlike file-sharing programs or messaging platforms, an LMS offers a single location where the lecturer and students can gain access to studying resources and complete studying activities without relying on various channels of communication. This is especially important for this research as the current approach of sharing learning materials at Federal University Lokoja is through several disconnected platforms that are supposed to be replaced by an integrated LMS.
From the outset of system design, accessibility refers to the ability for all users to access the same system without using an alternative version (Irizarry, 2026). The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) are globally recognized guidelines for creating accessible digital systems. Some of the features of these guidelines include alternative text for images, keyboard navigation, and compatibility with screen readers and other assistive technology (Ifenatuora, Awoyemi, & Atobatele, 2023). The WCAG principles are used to guide the design of the proposed system because this study is aimed at designing an accessible Learning Management System.
2.1.2 Historical Perspectives of the Subject Matter
The open-source Learning Management System most related to this study is Moodle, which was introduced in 2002, and was adopted for the FULokoja Moodle pilot. Since then, it has expanded to be one of the world's most popular open-source LMS platforms, with functions competitive to numerous commercial platforms (Salah & Thabet, 2021). From the mid-2010s, the use of LMS also rose quickly. In higher education, the acceptance of LMS platforms has grown, with Sanchez, Penarreta, and Soria Poma (2024) reporting that the number of users of a large online learning platform rose from twenty-six million in 2016 to one hundred and eighty-nine million in 2021.
Accessibility in Learning Management Systems has been developed more recently. Evaluation of LMS platforms according to the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) started gaining traction only in recent years, and research indicates that the accessibility level is still uneven across different platforms (Ifenatuora et al., 2023). This underscores the importance of thinking about accessibility at the design phase as opposed to as an extra add-on after a system is created.
2.1.3 Definition of the Specific Research Concept
Integrated Learning Management System is defined as a Learning Management System that integrates the key academic functions (course material upload, assignment submission, grading, announcement, and communication) and provides accessibility features as part of the development process. The system is created from the outset to support students with various needs and does not primarily provide accessibility as an afterthought. This approach is supported by Irizarry (2026) who concluded that accessibility features that are designed into the system during construction have a positive effect on usability for students with disabilities compared to accessibility features added after the system is constructed.
2.1.4 Discussion on the Study Area
In this research, two theoretical frameworks were applied. The first is technology acceptance, and this is why individuals accept and/or reject a new technology. Davis (1989) and Venkatesh, Morris, Davis and Davis (2003) proposed two models, namely the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), respectively, and these models indicate that the factors of interest include perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and the presence of support. In a review of sixty-eight research studies, Al-Nuaimi and Al-Emran (2021) identified these models as the most popular theories used in Learning Management System (LMS) adoption research.
The second theory is accessibility and universal design, which states that systems should be designed to accommodate people with varying abilities from the outset rather than adding in accessibility as an add-on. Ifenatuora et al. (2023) state that systems designed with universal design principles are generally easier to use for everyone, not only for people with disabilities. This research uses technology acceptance theory to gain insight into the problems of LMS adoption in FULokoja, and it takes universal design theory as the foundation to incorporate accessibility into the design of the proposed system.
2.2 Review of Relevant Approaches to the Research Area
Based on the literature reviewed, there are three general solutions that can be identified for accessibility enhancement in LMSs. The first option is to check whether the current LMS platform complies with accessibility standards like the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) and Section 508. Ifenatuora et al. (2023) compared Moodle, Blackboard, Canvas, and Google Classroom and determined that both Moodle and Canvas offered more accessibility features such as screen reader support, keyboard navigation, and video captioning. Blackboard and Google Classroom were slightly less accessible, particularly in alternative text for multimedia resources. The study also revealed that none of the platforms were fully usable for people with cognitive impairments.
The second is to directly test the Learning Management Systems with users with disabilities rather than on the basis of accessibility standards alone. El Koshiry et al. (2024) conducted an experiment with twenty blind postgraduate students using Moodle Cloud and found that the students who used the system showed significant improvement in their digital skills. This present study is significant because it examined accessibility with real users.
The third approach focuses on disability groups that have received less attention in previous research. Cinquin, Guitton, and Sauzéon (2019) found that most studies on accessible e-learning concentrate on visual and hearing impairments, while cognitive disabilities receive far less attention. Since this study focuses mainly on accessibility for students with visual, hearing, and mobility impairments, it does not claim to address cognitive accessibility, which remains an area requiring more in-depth research.
2.3 Review of Related Literature
2.3.1 Global LMS Adoption, Effectiveness, and Theoretical Models
Studies carried out in different parts of the world show that the adoption of Learning Management Systems has increased rapidly over the years, although their effectiveness still depends on how they are implemented. Sanchez et al. (2024) reviewed 45 LMS platforms and found that worldwide LMS usage is on the rise. Likewise, Salah and Thabet (2021) did a comparative study of twenty LMS and concluded that currently, open source LMS like Moodle, Chamilo, and Open edX offer functionalities similar to many costly commercial LMS. This makes them suitable options for institutions that demand a cost-effective learning platform.
While the technology is essential, there is more to successful implementation of a Learning Management System. Vasu et al. (2026) found that having a technically capable platform does not automatically lead to effective use. To support this view, Al-Nuaimi and Al-Emran (2021) reviewed 68 studies and found that the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) are still the two most widely used models to explain LMS adoption. They found that factors such as individual, organisational, and psychological factors determine user acceptance.
Ziraba, Akwene, Nkea and Lwanga (2020) also reported that technological, social, human and institutional supports are necessary for the successful adoption of Moodle. This indicates that for an LMS to be successful, institutions need to have the technology available and foster a culture that values the use of the technology by the lecturer and students. These findings are also significant for this study since they contribute to the understanding of the gradual implementation of the current Moodle pilot project in Federal University Lokoja and the development of the need for a system that is tailored to the needs of the institution.
2.3.2 LMS Adoption in Nigerian and African Higher Education
Research work done in Nigeria and other African countries revealed that LMS have been introduced by many institutions, but implementation and effective use of LMS differ from one institution to the other. According to Sulaiman and Abubakar (2024), the adoption of LMS enhances teaching effectiveness in five Nigerian universities, however, it faces challenges such as low digital literacy, weak internet connectivity, and inadequate institutional support. Likewise, Ojo (2024) noted that students at Ajayi Crowther University achieved better results when they learned with an LMS, but this platform was underutilized due to the lack of adequate training of some of the lecturers and also because there was no policy for some lecturers to use the platform regularly. Muntari and Abdullahi (2025) also stated that in order to improve education in Nigeria, one has to make changes in the education delivery system, not just minor changes.
Similar findings have also been reported in other Nigerian universities. The researchers Mohammed and Opeyemi (2020) found that the greatest discouragement of the lecturers of Ahmadu Bello University, Zaria to use Moodle was due to limited awareness and lack of technical support. In Imo State University, Oguguo et al. (2021) found that the students who adopted Moodle performed significantly better compared to those who adopted another computer-assisted learning package. Similarly, Eze et al. (2018) noted that the nature of the attitude of lecturers and the culture of the institution were the major obstacles in the use of e-learning facilities in a private university in Nigeria.
Other research has also found that digital infrastructure has an impact on successful implementation of LMS. Olanrewaju et al (2021) revealed that the students from the rural areas encountered greater difficulties during the COVID-19 pandemic due to the poor internet connection and limited availability of digital devices. In a similar way, Ajani, Govender, and Gamede (2025) found that the cost of data at a rural South African university negatively affected students' engagement with online teaching. On the other hand, Orji et al. (2025) reported better learning material and institutional support for the adoption of LMS in private secondary schools in Abuja. The results indicate that institutional and infrastructural problems are the primary ones for the LMS adoption.
Many of these findings also apply to the experience at Federal University Lokoja. The Computer Science Department adopted a Moodle-based Learning Management System in 2025 and trained twenty-three (23) lecturers to use the system (Adewumi, Ogbuju & Ihinkalu, 2025). Despite successful deployment and interest in the pilot from lecturers, there was still a need to get many lecturers to upload their course materials, and the project was predominantly centred on lecturer training, with little focus on student usability and accessibility. This serves as a good reminder that there is a need to have an integrated system that can help lecturers, and also enhance the overall learning experience for students, including those with disabilities.
2.3.3 Accessibility and Disability in LMS Design
Research on accessibility in LMS has been growing but has not been explored as much as the general LMS area. The study by Irizarry (2026) concluded that including accessibility supports in the design phase enhanced the usability of digital learning resources for students with disabilities. Likewise, El Koshiry et al. (2024) found that blind students who used a cloud-based LMS made significant improvements in their digital skills. Also, Ifenatuora et al. (2023) found that widely-used platforms like Moodle and Canvas still have accessibility issues, particularly within cognitive disabilities. Furthermore, Cinquin et al. (2019) noted that studies on cognitive accessibility are still lagging behind studies on other impairments, such as visual and hearing.
Other research has also revealed that accessibility is best addressed as part of the development of the system. Miedijensky and Haran (2026), Linhalis, Pavão and Silva (2026), and Baharin et al. (2026) all found that an accessible Learning Management System must be designed from the outset, rather than ensuring that accessibility is added in after the system is completed. These results confirm the approach used in the present study, where accessibility is considered one of the primary requirements of the design of the proposed system.
User studies with people with disabilities are also valuable in that they offer insights into problems faced by users with disabilities on digital learning platforms. Kırboyun Tipi (2023) concluded that students who are visually impaired can use screen readers to benefit from the devices, but still do not have access to scanned documents and photographed learning materials. Similarly, Lomellini, Lowenthal, Snelson and Trespalacios (2025) found that many institutions are still not adequately addressing the learning needs of students with disabilities and proposed the Universal Design for Learning approach as a remedy. Similarly, Priyadharsini and Sahaya Mary (2024) reported that visually impaired students faced difficulty accessing PDF and image-based documents while deaf students would need captions or subtitles to comprehend video information. These findings are directly reflected in the present study, which develops a system that will support students with disabilities through the addition of screen reader support, alternative text for images, and video captioning.
2.3.4 Faculty Attitudes Toward Disability and Inclusive Teaching
An Accessible Learning Management System will be successful only if lecturers can use it. Although accessibility features may be in place, if the lecturer is not aware of how to use such features, then they may not help students. Valle-Flórez, de Caso Fuertes, Baelo, and García-Martín (2021) determined that lecturers tended to have positive attitudes towards integrating students with disabilities, but felt they lacked practical expertise to create accessible learning materials. This is similar to what was experienced in the FULokoja Moodle pilot, where lecturers had to be trained to make effective use of the platform (Adewumi et al., 2025).
A review of twenty studies conducted by Fernández-Batanero, Montenegro-Rueda and Fernández-Cerero (2022) showed that students with disabilities still experience difficulties in accessing and participating in higher education, even when institutions claim to support inclusive education. This demonstrates that positive policies or attitudes, without action, are of little value. Considering this, the proposed system consists of an Accessibility Compliance Module that automatically analyzes learning materials for accessibility problems rather than relying solely on lecturers to remember the accessibility requirements.
2.3.5 Institutional and Policy Context
This focus on accessibility, in addition to technical concerns, is also embraced by national policy. The Discrimination Against Persons with Disabilities (Prohibition) Act, 2018 provides the right of persons with disabilities to access education equally. This serves as a legal instrument for the integration of accessibility in the proposed LMS and underscores the need for a more inclusive digital learning environment in Nigerian universities.
Additionally, Benjamen (2025) revealed that Learning Management Systems were also crucial in maintaining the continuity of teaching and learning during the COVID-19 pandemic. This benefit is especially critical for students who are unable to attend lectures due to disability, illness, remote location, transportation and power issues, or emergency situations. This reinforces the importance of a Learning Management System (LMS) that will enable learners to learn even when they are not physically at school.
2.3.6 Blended, Mobile, and Flexible Delivery Models
The study is relevant for students who are not necessarily able to attend lectures in person, and blended and mobile learning are relevant in this respect. Han (2023) created and tested a model for assessing the effectiveness of blended learning and concluded that course goals, learning materials, assessment, technological support, and the proportion of online and in-person learning experience all influence learning outcomes. These results indicate that a good LMS should enable both physical and online learning, rather than relying on one approach.
The importance of mobile learning cannot be underestimated because the majority of students at Federal University Lokoja use their mobile devices to access digital learning materials. Efiloğlu Kurt (2023) found that students are more likely to adopt mobile learning when the system is easy to use, and they believe it will improve their learning experience. These results are congruent with the technology acceptance model described earlier in this chapter and underscore the importance of creating a system that is user-friendly, mobile, easy and useful.
Ratinho and Martins (2023) also determined that transparency in feedback and learning progression positively influences students' engagement in digital learning environments. The present study does not employ gamification, but these results are of great importance for the development of an LMS that is easy to use and maintains students' attention. Thus, the proposed system is designed to work well on the low-cost smartphones which many students of FULokoja use, and is not supposed to depend on the assumption that every student has access to a laptop or desktop computer.
2.3.7 Nigerian Experience During and After the COVID-19 Disruption
The COVID-19 pandemic exposed many of the challenges affecting e-learning in Nigerian institutions and showed the need for more reliable digital learning systems. Ossai, Eze, Umeokonkwo, Izuagba, and Ogbonnaya (2023) observed that financial constraints, substandard internet connectivity, and low technical skills continued to affect medical students at Ebonyi State University even during the peak of the pandemic. The same was noted by Olanrewaju et al. (2021), who revealed that online learning was difficult for many learners due to low access to internet and the limited use of digital devices.
Consequently, Hassan, Dokoro, Jalo and Isah (2024) noted that there is widespread ICT integration among institutions, with significant variation between urban and rural schools, and between private and public schools. Ukpai (2021) had earlier suggested that the need for LMS integration into tertiary education existed prior to the pandemic, and that COVID-19 only highlighted it further. These studies demonstrate that LMSs need to be used beyond emergencies and should be part of regular teaching and learning.
However, none of the Nigerian studies reviewed in this section examined accessibility for students with disabilities. Most were addressing general issues and challenges of digital learning, internet connectivity, and digital devices, rather than questions that involve how students with disabilities access internet-based learning. This gap in the literature further justifies the focus of this study on the integration of accessibility into the design of the proposed Learning Management System (LMS) rather than treating it as a separate issue.

2.4 Summary of Research Findings and Knowledge Gap
The results of the literature search in this chapter revealed that the adoption of LMS is on the rise, not only globally but also in Nigeria. Having lecturers who are trained and an institution ready to support it has a positive impact on teaching effectiveness and student learning outcomes, as shown in the Nigerian and African studies reviewed above (Sulaiman & Abubakar, 2024; Ziraba et al., 2020). However, the problems highlighted by the studies carried out in Nigeria are low involvement of lecturers, lack of support from institutions, unstable internet, and low digital literacy. None of the studies cited from Nigeria or Africa, however, addressed any issue of accessibility such as captions for videos, screen readers, or other devices that enable students with disabilities to participate fully in digital learning. At the same time, studies in other countries show that although the majority of lecturers are supportive of inclusive education, students with disabilities are confronted with many barriers due to the lack of attention given to the accessibility of systems in their design. This indicates that there is a huge gap in the literature where studies related to the adoption of LMS and studies related to accessibility are conducted separately.
Similarly, this is the case at Federal University Lokoja. The university currently has the Computer Science Department's Moodle pilot, which was mostly about training and deployment, but it is not yet implemented across the entire university and has not yet been examined from an accessibility point of view. It also has FUL DeL, the Distance and E-Learning unit, which serves distance learners separately from on-campus students, with no public information on its accessibility provisions (Federal University Lokoja Distance and E-Learning, 2026). For the majority of students, academic communication and access to learning materials still rely on WhatsApp groups, photographed handouts, email, and Xender. None of these three efforts provide a single platform for learning and teaching.
The literature reviewed has shown that no previous study has proposed or adopted an integrated Moodle-based Learning Management System in a federal university in Nigeria that integrates core academic functions and accessibility from the outset of its design. This study attempts to fill that gap by incorporating the lessons taken from the FULokoja Moodle pilot and extending this to include accessibility and a student-centred approach to learning management.

CHAPTER THREE
SYSTEM ANALYSIS, DESIGN AND METHODOLOGY
3.1 System Analysis
System analysis is the process of studying an existing system to understand how it operates, identify its strengths and weaknesses, and determine the requirements for developing a better system. In this study, the analysis focuses on the current method of course delivery and academic communication at Federal University Lokoja. The findings from this analysis provide the basis for the design and implementation of the proposed Integrated Learning Management System presented in this chapter.
3.1.1 Outline of Problems with the Existing System
Academic communication and course delivery at Federal University Lokoja currently depend on three different systems, each with its own limitations. The first is the informal method used by most on-campus students, where learning materials are shared through printed handouts, WhatsApp groups, email, Xender, Bluetooth, or by taking photographs of lecture notes. This approach provides no central platform for learning and does not include any accessibility support (see Sections 1.1 and 1.2). The second is the Computer Science Department's Moodle pilot introduced in 2025. Although the pilot successfully deployed the platform and trained twenty-three lecturers, it is still limited to the department, has not been adopted across the university, and does not address accessibility (Adewumi, Ogbuju, & Ihinkalu, 2025). The third is the university's Distance and E-Learning (DeL) unit, which mainly serves distance and transfer students and does not publicly indicate the accessibility features available on its platform (Federal University Lokoja Distance and E-Learning, 2026).
None of these existing systems provides the general student population with a single, reliable, and accessible platform for learning. Learning materials are shared through different channels, making it difficult to ensure that every student receives the same information at the same time. There is also no unified platform where students can submit assignments, check grades, or communicate with lecturers outside lecture hours. In addition, videos are shared without captions, images do not include alternative text, and the existing arrangements are not designed with accessibility in mind. Since many FULokoja students depend on low-cost smartphones for internet access, the absence of a mobile-friendly and accessible system further limits effective learning, despite studies showing that ease of use on smartphones greatly influences students' willingness to adopt digital learning platforms (Efiloğlu Kurt, 2023).
3.1.2 Description of the Proposed System
The proposed system is a Moodle-based Integrated Learning Management System (ILMS) developed for Federal University Lokoja. Instead of building a separate application from scratch, the study extends the existing Moodle platform with custom features that are tailored to the academic and accessibility needs of the university. The system is designed to operate as a mobile-responsive learning environment suitable for the low-cost smartphones used by many students, while retaining Moodle's core strengths in course management, user roles, assessments, communication, and reporting.
The system combines course delivery, assignment submission and grading, announcements, student-lecturer communication, and accessibility support in a single academic environment. It also includes features such as screen-reader-compatible navigation, adjustable text size, high-contrast mode, and an Accessibility Compliance Module that checks uploaded materials for captions and alternative text before they are published. This design reflects the university's existing Moodle adoption while addressing the gap the pilot did not cover: student-centred accessibility and institution-specific academic workflow support.
The proposed system is intended to complement the existing Moodle pilot at Federal University Lokoja rather than replace it. While the pilot demonstrated that lecturers can be trained to use an LMS, this study extends that effort by focusing on usability, accessibility, and the broader management of academic activities. The proposed Moodle-based solution therefore builds on the progress already made by the university while addressing the areas that remain underdeveloped.

The system should perform reliably under low-bandwidth internet conditions, since unstable connectivity is common within the university environment. User passwords must be securely stored using a hashing algorithm, and role-based access control should be implemented to protect system resources. The user interface should also be fully responsive and optimised for smartphones, as these are the primary devices used by most students to access online learning.
3.2 System Design
3.2.1 Objectives of the Design
The design of the proposed Integrated Learning Management System is guided by five main objectives. First, accessibility is built into every part of the system from the beginning. Second, the system follows a mobile-first design because most FULokoja students access the internet using smartphones. Third, the system is designed to perform well even under unstable and low-bandwidth internet conditions commonly experienced within the university. Fourth, the Accessibility Compliance Module is designed separately from the main course management functions so that accessibility features can be improved or updated without affecting the rest of the system. Finally, the system uses role-based access control to ensure that students, lecturers, and administrators can only access the features assigned to their respective roles.
3.2.2 Architectural Design (System Architecture)
The proposed system follows a Moodle-based three-layer architecture consisting of the presentation layer, application layer, and data layer. In this architecture, Moodle forms the central platform and the custom project components are implemented as plugins and extensions layered on top of the core LMS. The Accessibility Compliance Module is developed as a separate Moodle component so that accessibility validation can be maintained independently of the general course management functions. This design keeps the system modular and easier to update without affecting the rest of the learning platform.

Figure 3.1: System Architecture of the Moodle-Based Integrated Learning Management System
The presentation layer consists of the standard Moodle interface, adapted through themes, templates, and custom page rendering to support mobile access and accessibility features such as screen-reader navigation, keyboard support, and adjustable text size. The application layer contains Moodle core services, role-based access control, plugin controllers, course and assignment logic, and the custom Accessibility Compliance Module that validates uploaded materials before publication. The data layer is supported by the Moodle database and file storage, where course records, user information, assignments, announcements, and uploaded learning materials are stored in a structured and secure manner.
This architecture is appropriate for Federal University Lokoja because it makes use of the institution's existing Moodle environment rather than creating a separate educational platform that would need to be maintained independently. It also aligns with the university's current teaching deployment while introducing a more student-centred and accessible academic experience.

3.2.3.2 Output Design
The system produces three primary outputs relevant to this discussion. The student dashboard output presents enrolled courses, upcoming assignment deadlines, and recent announcements in a single scrollable, screen-reader-navigable list, avoiding the fragmented view a student currently experiences across WhatsApp, email, and paper. The grade report output presents a student's submission status and grade per assignment, exportable as a plain-text summary for offline reference given intermittent connectivity. The accessibility compliance report, available to administrators, lists the proportion of uploaded materials that passed the caption and alt-text check automatically versus those flagged for lecturer correction, giving the university a concrete, ongoing measure of accessibility compliance that none of the three existing systems described in Section 3.1.1 currently produce.

3.2.4.2 Activity Diagram
Figure 3.3 shows the accessibility compliance workflow specifically, since this is the single feature that most distinguishes this project from the existing FULokoja Moodle pilot and from FUL DeL, neither of which performs this check.

When a lecturer uploads material, the system first identifies the file type. Video files are checked for an attached caption or transcript file; documents and images are checked for alternative text. If either check fails, the lecturer is prompted to add the missing element before the material is published; if the check passes, or if the material is already in an inherently accessible plain-text format, the material is published immediately and enrolled students are notified.

3.2.5 User Interface Design
3.2.5.1 Main Menu Designs
The Student main menu presents five items: Courses, Assignments, Announcements, Messages, and Accessibility Settings, in that order, reflecting the frequency with which students are expected to use each function. The Lecturer main menu presents My Courses, Upload Material, Grade Submissions, Post Announcement, and Messages. 3.2.7 Application Algorithm
3.2.7.1 Pseudocode
Algorithm 1 below governs user authentication.
BEGIN Login(inputId, inputPassword)
user <- FIND User WHERE email = inputId OR matricNumber = inputId
IF user IS NULL THEN
RETURN error "Account not found"
END IF
IF NOT VerifyHash(inputPassword, user.passwordHash) THEN
RETURN error "Incorrect password"
END IF
session <- CreateSession(user.userId, user.role)
RETURN session
END

Algorithm 2 governs the accessibility compliance check triggered on material upload, matching the activity diagram in Figure 3.3.
BEGIN CheckAccessibility(material)
IF material.fileType = "video" THEN
IF material.captionFile IS NULL THEN
material.accessibilityStatus <- "flagged"
NotifyLecturer("Add captions before publishing")
RETURN
END IF
ELSE IF material.fileType IN ("image", "document") THEN
IF material.altText IS EMPTY THEN
material.accessibilityStatus <- "flagged"
NotifyLecturer("Add alternative text before publishing")
RETURN
END IF
END IF
material.accessibilityStatus <- "passed"
Publish(material)
NotifyEnrolledStudents(material.courseId)
END

Algorithm 3 governs assignment submission and grade release.
BEGIN SubmitAssignment(studentId, assignmentId, file)
assignment <- FIND Assignment WHERE assignmentId = assignmentId
IF CurrentTime() > assignment.dueDate THEN
RETURN error "Deadline has passed"
END IF
submission <- CREATE Submission(studentId, assignmentId, file, submittedAt = CurrentTime())
RETURN confirmation
END

BEGIN ReleaseGrade(submissionId, gradeValue)
submission <- FIND Submission WHERE submissionId = submissionId
submission.grade <- gradeValue
NotifyStudent(submission.studentId, "Grade released")
END
3.3 Methodology
3.3.1 Description of the Selected Methodology
This project adopts the Agile methodology, structured as a repeating six-stage cycle: plan, design, develop, test, review, and deploy, shown in Figure 3.6. Each full cycle corresponds to one sprint, delivering one working, testable increment of the system rather than attempting to build the entire system in one long, unbroken phase. In this study, the Agile approach is adapted to Moodle plugin development so that each sprint adds a functional extension to the existing LMS rather than building an entirely separate system from scratch.

Figure 3.6: Agile Development Cycle Adopted for this Project
Source: https://luvina.net/
Four sprints were used to build the Moodle-based ILMS. Sprint one focused on configuring the Moodle environment, defining the project scope, and identifying the plugin structure needed for students, lecturers, and administrators. Sprint two delivered course material upload together with the Accessibility Compliance Module described in Section 3.2.2, intentionally paired together because accessibility validation was treated as part of the upload process rather than as an afterthought. Sprint three delivered assignment management, grading support, and student progress visibility. Sprint four delivered announcements, messaging, accessibility preferences, and the testing and review stage described in Chapter Four.
3.3.2 Justification for the Selected Methodology
Agile was chosen over the Waterfall model and over Rapid Application Development because the project is built on a platform that already contains core LMS functionality. Instead of rebuilding infrastructure that Moodle already provides, the study focuses on adding new features and improving usability in an iterative manner. This is especially important because accessibility issues, mobile usability, and low-bandwidth performance often become visible only after real testing with users, not at the beginning of design.
Because accessibility testing depends on genuine feedback from real users, and because, as discussed in Section 1.6, this project was only able to reach a small number of students with mobility-related challenges during development, an iterative approach allowed each accessibility feature to be revised in a later sprint as feedback became available. Waterfall's rigid phases would have made such revisions more difficult, since accessibility issues discovered late would have required reopening an already-closed design stage. Agile also suits the realities of FULokoja, where internet instability and the predominance of mobile access affect usage patterns and require continuous refinement.
In addition, the Moodle-based approach is more realistic for a final-year project because it permits the development team to focus on problem-specific innovation rather than re-creating authentication systems, course structures, user roles, and content management features that Moodle already handles effectively. The project therefore uses Agile as a practical framework for extending Moodle to meet institutional needs in a controlled, testable, and maintainable way.
